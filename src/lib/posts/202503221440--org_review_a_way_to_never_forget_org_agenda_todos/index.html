1. 2.1

1.1. Introduction

In the last few months I've revamped my org-agenda workflow to a state I've never been more satisfied with. I'll share here how org-review1 made it possible.

1.2. The ideal: Separating "now" and "very soon" from everything else

In my view, the primary purpose of a "task management" is system is to show you only the tasks you need to be shown, while hiding the rest until it becomes urgent to do them, their importance to other tasks rise, or you simply feel like doing them now. As such, we should distinguish tasks which are relevant to "now" and "very soon" from everything else: "later," "never," and "unsure when."

I'd bet (i) is a familiar and intuitive ideal for most Emacs users since we tend to be the type to think about the workflows/systems we use and how we might reshape them to suit our needs better. An org-agenda without (i) is like a workflow where one simply calls M-x org-agenda t ("List of all todo entries"). It'll be obvious to those who have hundreds or even thousands of todos how seeing a gigantic running list of all your tasks doesn't help. We need to fulfill (i) because, otherwise, the looking at your todo list to decide what task you need to do now becomes a task in itself—and that means we expend time and energy figuring out what to do rather than doing what needs to be done.

1.6. 1: Occlusion

As such, an ideal org-agenda means (i) occluding (hiding) or separating tasks non-relevant to the present.

There are two common straightforward ways to fulfill (i):

  1. Having an "inbox." An inbox separates tasks which one has already committed to from ones they haven't yet committed to.
  2. Add scheduling and deadline information to tasks and occlude (hide) them until they approach. This is to delay visibility of tasks until a date more appropriate to attending to that task.2

I have used and continue to use both strategies to occlude tasks.

1.7. 2: Prioritization

However, those two strategies are not enough. Even with an inbox and hiding tasks until their scheduled/deadline date approaches, I still had dozens (at least) of todos simultaneously visible in my org-agenda views. I committed to these tasks, but there was a problem: although I committed to all these tasks, when considering factors such as urgency, life-priorities, mood, and time available, only a small portion of these tasks were ones I should be worked on now. The rest were ones that I could worry about later, whether next week, two weeks from now, a month from now, or some ambiguous, yet-to-be-determined point in the future.

Consequently, I naturally moved to crafting an org-agenda practice reliant on org-agenda's built-in priorities and effort estimates. I had previously used priorities and time efforts sporadically, but

My previous org-agenda set up tried to cut down the number of non-inbox todos by abusing the scheduling functionality of org. The functionality of deadlines remained as intended, but I re-tooled scheduling in an atypical way: a task's scheduled date was the date I wanted to see that task again. In my org-agenda views, the tasks meant for "now" were the ones whose scheduled date was today or in the past. There was flexibility in the method, since I could also use the scheduled date as simply a date I wanted to check back in with a todo, deciding whether I should move it to the inbox, do it now, or defer it again. (Of course, abusing scheduling like this meant there was no longer an equivalent to the typical schedule-deadline difference.)

Effectively, this was a way to snooze3 tasks until later—that is, I would defer tasks, which is a temporal way to occlude todos. I ended up sticking with this workflow for over a year.

But over time, I discovered a significant issue. I realized that (i) wasn't enough: there was no way to tell how long a todo sat in my agenda, waiting for me to attend to it. As such, although I could hide a task for another day, once it became visible and I was actively working on it, if I forgot about the task or de-prioritized it, there was nothing in the system that would tell me that too much time had passed since I was last productive on that task. Consequently, there were many todos that fell through my workflow because I started work on them, did not complete them, and the time I should've had it completed by had passed. (Strictly speaking, one could use deadlines for this purpose, but I never could find a way for such deadlined todos to be prominent nor distinguished from other deadlines used in the typical "this is an actual deadline" way.)

Therefore, in addition to (i), there is (ii): visible (non-occluded) tasks should have a way of indicating to me that they should be checked on.

In sum, there should be a way to occlude tasks (i.e., make a todo visible until a specified date)—this is (i)---while also indicating a certain date when my future self should be concerned it is not yet completed (i.e., specify another date for reviewing a todo) whether to re-defer it, to move it the inbox, or to start working on again—this is (ii).

1.8. 3: Reviewing

1.8.1. Two org-agenda views

Retaining (i) while achieving (ii) is what I have made perfect use of org-review for. My current org-agenda is much more effective and simpler, not requiring a re-conceptualization of org's scheduling functionality.

Currently, I have (in outline) two org agenda views.

  1. One org-agenda view for tasks whose scheduled or deadline date is today (or in the past) and other tasks (non-schedule, non-deadline) that I'm either working on now or will work on soon—the urgency and priority of these tasks are determined using org priorities and effort estimates, although it's ultimately up to me what I choose to work on any given day.4 This is my main, "time to be productive" org-agenda view.
  2. The other org agenda view shows my "inbox" and "to-review tasks"---this is where org-review comes in. A task tagged with "inbox" is in the inbox org-agenda block until I call org-review-insert-next-review on it, selecting the date on which this task will reappear as a task to review.5 (Alternatively, I can just remove the inbox tag and this task will appear in my first agenda view: it is a task that I am working on now or will work on soon. In these cases, I also call org-review-insert-next-review, or org-review-insert-last-review which automatically sets the NEXT_REVIEW property value if org-review-sets-next-date is non-nil. I explain why later.) In this same agenda view, I have another org-agenda block for tasks whose NEXT_REVIEW date is today or in the past.6 It is here that I decide what to do with a task. Do I move it to my main, first agenda view by removing the inbox tag? Do I call org-review-insert-next-review again to re-defer it? Or do I remove its NEXT_REVIEW property because I'm no longer sure whether or when this task should be deferred to.

1.8.2. Org-review: Immediately identify which tasks need addressing

These two views, however, are not uniquely made possible by org-review. In fact, I had very similar views when I used scheduling instead of org-review's org-review-insert-next-review: instead of filtering by the date in the NEXT_REVIEW property, I would filter by the scheduled date. (Although, of course, using NEXT_REVIEW instead of scheduling allows actually scheduled tasks to always appear in my main agenda view, which is a plus.) The benefit unique to org-review is my usage of the NEXT_REVIEW property in tasks that are also in my main agenda view. That is, with org-review, I can set a date in which tasks I've set out to do "now or soon" can be reviewed (without using schedules or deadlines, which would remove the possibility of using schedules or deadlines for other purposes). I do this with another org-agenda block in that agenda view, showing tasks that aren't in the inbox and whose NEXT_REVIEW date is today or passed.

This solves the issue of (ii), that tasks need to be done soon otherwise too many pile up and either require long sessions of reviewing todos or important or urgent todos to pass by with you realizing (tk is this the essence of it?). The tasks in the to-review agenda block in my main view are those which my past-self determined should be checked on (put back on the inbox, re-prioritized, cancelled, etc.) by now. So, when I fall behind in work, I can review the tasks in the to-review block and not worry about having important or urgent tasks slip by me—the non-schedule, non-deadline tasks in this view but not in that block are ones that aren't in-need of review now, and thus are fine not having been done already.

There are many more details to how I use org-agenda, but I hope this write-up explains my usage of org-review in a way that might've shown you a way to use the package you haven't already thought of.
